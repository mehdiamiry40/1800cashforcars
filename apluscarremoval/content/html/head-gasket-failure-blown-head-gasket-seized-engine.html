A blown head gasket is one of those repairs that car owners often don’t take seriously until the damage is already done. The early warning signs — a slight rise in the temperature gauge, a small coolant loss, a faint sweet smell from the exhaust — are easy to dismiss or delay. But ignoring them can quickly turn a fixable problem into an engine rebuild or a complete write-off.

This guide covers what a head gasket failure actually looks like, what causes it to progress into a seized engine, what repair costs look like in Australia, and — if the repair isn’t worth it — what your practical options are.

What Does a Head Gasket Actually Do?

The head gasket sits between the engine block (the lower half of the engine containing the pistons and cylinders) and the cylinder head (the upper section containing the valves and camshaft). Its job is to seal that joint under pressure while keeping three separate things completely separate: engine coolant, engine oil, and the combustion gases created when the fuel fires.

When the gasket is intact, each of these stays in its own circuit. When it fails — through heat damage, age, or mechanical stress — those boundaries break down. Coolant can enter the oil. Combustion gases can enter the cooling system. Oil can leak externally. What starts as a small breach tends to get significantly worse with continued driving.

Common Signs of a Blown Head Gasket

Some of these symptoms can also point to other issues, so a mechanic’s diagnosis is always worth getting before assuming the worst. That said, these are the signs most commonly associated with head gasket failure:

  • White or grey smoke from the exhaust — particularly on a warm engine; this typically indicates coolant is burning in the combustion chamber
  • Milky or frothy oil — check the dipstick and under the oil filler cap; a milky, chocolate-milkshake appearance indicates coolant mixing with oil
  • Unexplained coolant loss — the reservoir drops but there are no obvious external leaks under the car
  • Engine overheating — recurring or sudden temperature spikes, especially if the heater also stops producing warm air
  • Bubbles in the overflow or coolant reservoir — combustion gases entering the cooling system
  • Rough idle or misfire — if coolant enters a cylinder, it affects combustion and can feel like a misfire
  • Sweet smell from the exhaust or cabin heater — the smell of burning coolant (ethylene glycol has a distinctive sweet odour)
  • Compression loss — a cylinder losing pressure; this requires a compression test to identify
  • Hard starting — particularly on cold mornings if coolant is seeping into cylinders overnight
  • Heater blowing cold — if coolant is being lost from the system, the heater core may not have enough flow
  • Temperature or coolant warning light on the dash

⚠ If your temperature gauge rises or the coolant warning light appears while driving, pull over safely, turn the engine off, and arrange a tow. Continuing to drive an overheating vehicle can turn a repair into a write-off within minutes.

Head Gasket Leak vs Fully Blown Head Gasket

Not all head gasket issues are the same. There’s a meaningful difference between a small external seep and a full internal failure — and the difference matters for repair cost and urgency.

  • External seep — coolant or oil appears on the outside of the engine near the head/block join. Often slow to develop; can sometimes be monitored short-term.
  • Internal coolant leak — coolant enters the cylinders or mixes with oil internally. No external drip, but the engine is being damaged with every start.
  • Combustion gas in the cooling system — the most serious failure mode; hot combustion gases enter the coolant circuit, causing rapid overheating and coolant loss.
  • Oil and coolant mixing — can cause catastrophic lubrication failure if driven.

Early diagnosis matters because a minor external leak caught early is a very different (and much cheaper) repair than a warped head caused by repeated overheating. A combustion leak test or pressure test by a mechanic can confirm the failure type before you commit to a repair bill.

How Ignoring Early Warnings Can Lead to a Seized Engine

This is where the consequences become serious. The progression from a head gasket leak to a seized engine typically follows a predictable path:

  1. Coolant loss — the breach allows coolant to escape into the cylinders or combustion chamber, reducing the coolant level in the system.
  2. Overheating — with reduced coolant, the engine can’t maintain operating temperature. Heat builds.
  3. Warped cylinder head — aluminium heads warp under sustained heat stress, making the original gasket failure worse and potentially making a simple reseal impossible.
  4. Oil contamination — if coolant mixes with oil, the lubricating properties of the oil break down. The oil becomes a milky, viscous liquid that doesn’t protect the engine properly.
  5. Bearing and component damage — running with contaminated oil damages the crankshaft bearings, piston rings, cylinder walls, and other moving parts.
  6. Seizure — in severe cases, a lack of lubrication causes metal-on-metal contact, generating enough friction and heat to weld or lock components in place. The engine physically cannot turn over.

Once an engine seizes, the repair options narrow to an engine replacement or a full rebuild — both of which are significantly more expensive than the original head gasket repair would have been.

Head Gasket Repair Cost in Australia (Reality Check)

Head gasket repairs are labour-intensive and the total cost depends heavily on what the mechanic finds when the head comes off. There is no simple flat rate.

Factors that push the cost up include:

  • Engine layout — transversely mounted engines in front-wheel drive vehicles often require significant component removal just to access the head
  • Aluminium heads — common in modern engines; aluminium warps more easily and may require machining (resurfacing) or replacement
  • Labour hours — a basic job might take 8–12 hours; a complex engine can take considerably longer
  • Timing components — many head gasket repairs require the timing chain or belt to be disturbed, which typically means replacing those components at the same time
  • Cooling system damage — if overheating has cracked the head, damaged the radiator, or failed the water pump or thermostat, those components are added to the bill
  • Bore or valve damage — if coolant entered cylinders and caused hydraulic lock (trying to compress liquid), further internal damage is possible

In Australia, a straightforward head gasket repair on a common four-cylinder engine typically runs into several thousand dollars once labour, machining, coolant, gasket sets, and associated components are included. For larger or more complex engines, the figure can be considerably higher. Always get a written quote with a full parts and labour breakdown before authorising work.

When Is It Not Worth Fixing?

A head gasket repair is a significant investment. There are situations where it makes financial sense, and situations where it doesn’t. Consider the following:

  • The car’s market value is less than the repair cost — if the car in working order would sell for $4,000 and the repair quote is $5,500, the numbers don’t stack up
  • The engine has already seized — repair now means an engine replacement or rebuild, not just a gasket; the cost brackets jump significantly
  • Multiple other issues are also present — worn tyres, suspension problems, failing transmission, or brake work that also needs doing compounds the calculus
  • Very high kilometres — even after a head gasket repair, other age-related issues remain
  • Recurring overheating — if the car has overheated multiple times, there may be broader cooling system or engine integrity issues beyond the gasket
  • Unregistered or rego due — adding registration costs to a large repair bill may not be worthwhile

Example: If your car is worth $3,500 in working condition and the mechanic’s quote — including head machining, timing belt, and water pump — comes to $4,800, selling the car as damaged and putting that money toward a replacement may be the more practical decision.

Makes and Engines Commonly Discussed for Head Gasket Issues

Head gasket failure can occur in any vehicle if the cooling system is neglected or the engine is subjected to sustained overheating. That said, some engines and models come up more frequently in owner forums and mechanic discussions:

  • Subaru EJ-series (non-turbo) — the EJ20 and EJ25 non-turbo engines fitted to Liberty, Forester, Impreza, and Outback models from the late 1990s through mid-2000s are frequently discussed for head gasket concerns, particularly around the 150,000–200,000 km mark
  • Holden Cruze (1.8L petrol) — overheating and head gasket issues are commonly reported by Cruze owners, often related to cooling system components
  • High-km European vehicles — many European engines use aluminium construction throughout; labour rates and parts costs make head gasket repairs on these vehicles expensive
  • Any vehicle with a neglected cooling system — regardless of brand or model, a cooling system that hasn’t been serviced (old coolant, worn thermostat, ageing hoses, failed radiator cap) is at higher risk

If your car is on this list, it doesn’t mean failure is inevitable — it means paying attention to cooling system condition and early symptoms is particularly worthwhile.

Can You Drive With a Blown Head Gasket?

The short answer is no — not safely and not without risking significantly more damage.

Even if the car appears to be running normally at first, continuing to drive with a known or suspected head gasket issue means the engine is being damaged with every kilometre. Coolant loss leads to overheating. Overheating leads to a warped head. A warped head leads to an engine that can no longer be saved with a gasket replacement alone.

If you suspect a head gasket issue, have the car towed to a mechanic for diagnosis rather than driving it. The tow cost is a fraction of what further engine damage will add to the repair bill.

Do Head Gasket Sealers Work?

Head gasket sealers — liquid additives poured into the cooling system — are marketed as a quick fix for minor leaks. They can temporarily seal very small external seeps in some cases, but they come with significant limitations:

  • They are not effective for internal leaks, oil-coolant mixing, or combustion gas ingress into the cooling system
  • They can clog small passages in the cooling system, heater core, or radiator, causing new problems
  • They do not address the underlying cause — the gasket is still failing
  • Mechanics generally do not recommend them as anything more than a short-term stopgap on a low-value vehicle heading for disposal

If you’re considering a sealer, it’s worth being honest about what you’re trying to achieve. If the goal is to buy a little time to get the car to a buyer or removal service, that’s a different situation to trying to fix the problem long-term.

How to Reduce the Risk (Prevention)

Head gasket failure is often preventable with regular cooling system attention. These are the areas that matter most:

  • Fix coolant leaks promptly — even small external leaks reduce the system’s ability to manage heat
  • Use the correct coolant type and concentration for your vehicle — check your owner’s manual
  • Replace the thermostat and radiator pressure cap at recommended intervals — both are cheap parts that can cause significant damage if they fail
  • Have the water pump inspected at timing belt service intervals — water pump failure causes immediate overheating
  • Flush and replace coolant on schedule — old coolant loses its anti-corrosion properties, which can damage the water pump, radiator, and head
  • Watch the temperature gauge — if it moves out of its normal range, investigate immediately
  • Check oil and coolant levels regularly — unexplained drops are early warning signs

What Happens When an Engine Seizes? (Repair vs Sell)

When an engine seizes, the practical repair options are an engine replacement or a full internal rebuild. Both are time-consuming and expensive — and come with their own risks.

  • A used replacement engine carries unknown history — it may have its own issues, and there’s no guarantee of longevity
  • A full rebuild is skilled labour-intensive and parts-heavy; on older vehicles, the cost often approaches or exceeds the car’s value
  • Even after repair, the vehicle’s market value reflects its history

On the other side of the ledger: a seized engine doesn’t mean the car has zero value. There is still scrap metal, recyclable aluminium and steel, and potentially serviceable parts (gearbox, doors, suspension, interior) that retain value to wreckers and recyclers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the first signs of a blown head gasket?

Common early signs include white or grey exhaust smoke on a warm engine, milky oil on the dipstick or under the oil cap, unexplained coolant loss without visible leaks, engine overheating, and a rough idle or misfire. A sweet smell from the exhaust or heater can also indicate coolant is burning.

Is a blown head gasket worth fixing?

It depends on the car’s age, kilometres, overall condition, and the repair cost relative to market value. If repair costs exceed what the car is worth — or if the engine has already overheated and warped — selling as damaged is often the more practical path. A written quote from a mechanic gives you the clearest basis for the decision.

Can a blown head gasket cause a seized engine?

Yes. If a failing head gasket allows coolant into the engine oil, the oil loses its lubricating properties. Running an engine with contaminated or insufficient oil can damage bearings, rings, and pistons — and in severe cases, cause the engine to seize completely.

Can I sell a car with a blown head gasket?

Can I drive my car if it is overheating?

No. Continuing to drive an overheating vehicle can cause rapid and severe engine damage within minutes — including a warped head or seized engine. Pull over, turn the engine off, and arrange a tow.

Does milky oil always mean a blown head gasket?

Not always. Milky or foamy oil can also be caused by condensation in short-trip driving, particularly in colder conditions. However, milky oil combined with coolant loss and overheating is a strong indicator. A mechanic should perform a pressure test and combustion gas check to confirm the cause.
